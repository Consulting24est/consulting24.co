Crypto Exchange Licensing Checklist: 5 Areas to Assess Before Launch

By Mardo Soo, Founder & CEO, Consulting24 (X24Consulting OÜ) · Updated 2026-10-24

Short answer: Use this crypto exchange licensing checklist to evaluate your operating model, target markets, compliance, and entity structure before your exchange launch.

Use a crypto exchange licensing checklist to map your platform’s activities before choosing a jurisdiction. A spot trading platform, an OTC desk, custody services, fiat conversion, and token issuance can raise different licensing questions, even within the same market.

It’s reasonable to want a clear answer to “Which licence do we need?” But that answer depends on what the exchange will actually do and where its customers are based. This checklist helps you identify the activities and customer markets that need regulatory analysis, compare jurisdiction-specific obligations without treating them as interchangeable, and prepare evidence before beginning an application.

The five areas cover your operating model, target markets, jurisdiction and entity structure, governance and compliance readiness, and supporting application evidence. For each area, document what the exchange does, who is responsible, and what evidence is available. This will help you spot gaps, set launch priorities, and plan next steps around the exchange’s actual scope. Map the activities first, then assess the licensing route.

Key Takeaways

  • Use the crypto exchange licensing checklist to separate spot trading, fiat services, custody, OTC, derivatives, and token issuance.
  • Build a jurisdiction matrix around customer locations and services, not just the company’s incorporation address.

  • Organize application materials by activity, control, and accountable owner, using the sequence as a planning aid rather than a universal filing order.
  • Turn the completed checklist into a scoped licensing work plan aligned with your exchange model and target markets.

Crypto exchange licensing checklist: define exactly what your platform does

Start with the business activities, not a preferred jurisdiction or licence label. A platform described as an “exchange” may match customer orders, trade as principal, route orders to another venue, hold assets, or combine several functions. These operating differences shape the regulatory questions to assess in each jurisdiction.

An activity-led licensing assessment maps what the platform does, who it serves, and how assets and transactions move before identifying relevant regulatory requirements.

Use this crypto exchange licensing checklist to create a working inventory. For each function, record the customer action, the entity involved, the asset or funds handled, and the point at which the platform exercises control. Background on cryptocurrency exchange operation and regulation can help clarify common exchange models, but it isn’t a substitute for jurisdiction-specific analysis.

Inventory exchange functions, funds, and customer touchpoints

Describe how a trade is executed. Does the platform match orders from buyers and sellers, take the other side as counterparty, or route orders to an external venue? Note whether customers can trade only crypto-to-crypto or also use fiat on-ramps and off-ramps. Record who receives fiat, who settles the transaction, and which legal entity provides each step.

Then trace customer assets from deposit to withdrawal. Identify whether the platform or another party controls private keys, who can authorize withdrawals, and how assets are held while a trade is pending. Map the customer journey from account opening through trading and withdrawal. List the countries where customers can access the service or are actively targeted. These details make the operating model concrete and help distinguish a technical feature from a service the business actually provides.

Separate adjacent services from the core exchange

Don’t fold every feature into “exchange.” Assess each service on its own facts, because related activities can raise different licensing questions across jurisdictions. A single interface can combine activities that regulators may treat differently.

  • Spot trading: Record whether the platform matches trades, acts as counterparty, or routes orders.
  • Fiat on-ramps and off-ramps: Map payment instructions, settlement flows, and the parties handling customer funds.
  • Custody: State who controls keys and assets, and who approves withdrawals.
  • OTC dealing: Document how customers request quotes, who sets prices, and which entity executes each trade.
  • Derivatives: Identify the products offered, the transaction flow, and the legal entity responsible.
  • Staking and token issuance: Separate the service terms, customer relationship, and issuing or operating entity from exchange functions.

For every adjacent service, capture its customer group, transaction flow, asset control points, and responsible legal entity. Don’t assume an exchange authorization automatically covers custody, staking, OTC dealing, derivatives, or token issuance. Treat this inventory as the factual baseline for the jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction review that follows.

Check customer markets and jurisdiction before selecting a crypto exchange licence

Incorporation is only one part of the analysis. A company’s registered address doesn’t, by itself, establish which rules apply to the exchange’s services or where it can serve customers. Treat incorporation, licensing, tax, and customer access as separate questions, then assess how they connect to your operating model.

Jurisdiction choice follows the actual activity and target market, not the company’s address alone. For each proposed market, identify the services offered, how customers can access them, which regulator is relevant, and what current rules need review. A jurisdiction matrix lets you compare markets consistently without assuming that one country’s requirements transfer to another.

Assess where customers can access the exchange

Map intended customer countries, restricted markets, and planned marketing channels. Include app stores, affiliates, social media, local-language campaigns, and direct sales if they form part of the launch plan. The aim is to document how the exchange could reach customers, not just where its team or company is based.

For each country, assess whether local rules consider customer location, active solicitation, service delivery, or other factors. Don’t treat a website block or geolocation setting as a complete legal analysis. Access controls can support a market policy, but they don’t decide by themselves whether the exchange’s activity triggers local requirements.

Compare jurisdictions without assuming passporting

Use one row per jurisdiction and compare consistent factors: service scope, customer access, regulator, authorization process, ongoing supervision, application evidence, and operational requirements. The FATF risk-based approach for virtual asset service providers is a useful international reference for understanding the risk-based AML/CFT framework, but it doesn’t replace local rules or determine authorization in a specific country.

Keep market access and entity formation distinct. A structure that supports operations in one jurisdiction doesn’t automatically authorize customer activity elsewhere. In particular, Panama does not provide EU passporting. If EU customers are part of the plan, assess the applicable EU authorization pathway separately rather than assuming a Panama entity grants access across the bloc. The Panama crypto company registration guide explains the company formation context, which should be evaluated against the exchange’s activities and intended markets.

Before shortlisting jurisdictions, mark each target market as planned, restricted, or requiring further analysis. Then attach the relevant service, customer-access plan, regulator, and open legal questions. This turns broad expansion goals into specific decisions for counsel and management. Review compliance, governance, and technology controls before filing

A policy document is not proof that a control works. Before filing, compare written procedures with operational evidence, such as onboarding records, review logs, escalation decisions, access records, and test results. The right evidence depends on the exchange’s activities, architecture, and target jurisdiction, so treat this as a readiness review rather than a universal list of legal obligations.

For each control, record its owner, how often it operates, where evidence is retained, and who reviews exceptions. Mark jurisdiction-specific requirements for legal review. The ESMA MiCA authorization framework is relevant to EU authorization and supervision; it should not be applied as a template for every market.

Prepare AML, customer due diligence, and monitoring controls

Map the customer journey from application through account activity and exit. Describe how identity information is collected and reviewed, how customer or transaction risk is assessed, and what triggers enhanced review or account restrictions. Document how the process changes for different customer types or services, where applicable under the rules being assessed.

Next, show how potentially concerning activity moves from detection to decision. A written procedure should identify who reviews alerts, how cases are escalated, how decisions are recorded, and which reporting or retention duties require jurisdiction-specific legal assessment. Don’t present a monitoring tool or policy as effective evidence on its own. Prepare examples of completed reviews, recorded outcomes, and access-controlled case records where available.

Make accountability visible. Name the role responsible for each control, the backup or escalation route, and the manager who can resolve exceptions. The crypto exchange licensing checklist should connect each procedure to its owner and evidence, so reviewers can see how it operates rather than only what the policy says.

Document governance, cybersecurity, and customer safeguards

Describe the controls that match the platform’s actual architecture. For custody, map key management, permissions, withdrawal approval, and separation of duties. For systems and vendors, document access controls, incident response, recovery arrangements, and how operational dependencies are reviewed. Avoid generic claims such as “assets are secure.” Specify the process, accountable team, and available records.

  • Governance: Identify which policies and risk decisions require board or senior-management approval, and retain approval records.
  • Security and continuity: Link system access, key-management procedures, incident handling, and recovery tests to the systems and custodial model in use.
  • Customer safeguards: Document complaints handling, customer disclosures, conflicts procedures, and how customer assets are managed.
  • Evidence: Keep versions, review dates, test outcomes, incident records, and remediation actions together with the relevant policy.

Use this inventory to identify gaps before assembling an application. A control may be well designed but not yet tested, or operating in practice without a clear owner or retained evidence. Record each gap, its accountable decision-maker, and whether it requires operational work or jurisdiction-specific legal review. That distinction helps management set filing priorities without treating one jurisdiction’s standards as universal.

Use this crypto exchange licensing checklist to assemble application evidence

Build the evidence pack around the exchange you’ve mapped, not a generic application template. The sequence below is a planning aid, not a universal filing order. Application formats and submission requirements vary by jurisdiction. For every document, record the activity it supports, the control it demonstrates, and the person responsible for keeping it accurate.

Build a coherent application evidence pack

Group materials by business, governance, operations, and technology. Keep a document index with the file name, version, owner, related service, and evidence purpose. This makes it easier to trace each claim to a source and identify missing or outdated materials before filing.

  1. Entity and ownership: Gather incorporation and ownership records, group structure, organizational charts, and leadership biographies. Link each entity to its role in the exchange and to the activities it will perform.
  2. Business model: Prepare the business plan, product descriptions, customer journey, and revenue flows. Show how a customer moves from onboarding to trading, deposits, and withdrawals, and identify which entity is responsible at each step.
  3. Activity and control map: Connect each service to the controls that support it. For example, link custody to key-management and withdrawal procedures, and link fiat flows to the relevant settlement process and accountable owner.
  4. Policies and risk assessments: Organize compliance, customer due diligence, complaints, conflicts, and risk documents. Include approval records and identify which service, control, and jurisdiction each document addresses.
  5. Technology and operational evidence: Assemble architecture descriptions, access-control records, incident procedures, continuity materials, and available control test results. Make clear which systems and teams each item covers.

Don’t rely on a policy title to establish readiness. Pair written procedures with operational evidence where available, such as review records, approvals, test outcomes, and remediation tracking. If a control is planned but not yet operating, label it accurately and assign an owner and next action.

Run a pre-submission consistency check

Review the pack as one account of the business. Compare application descriptions with product functions, customer terms, process documents, and organizational records. A feature described as non-custodial, for example, should align with documented asset and key-control arrangements. Resolve differences before they become unsupported claims.

  • Check that entity names, ownership details, roles, and service responsibilities match across documents.
  • Trace customer funds and assets through each diagram, policy, and product description.
  • Flag missing evidence, conflicting statements, and assumptions that need jurisdiction-specific verification.
  • Record each open issue, its decision-maker, evidence owner, and resolution status.

A concise issue log gives management a clear view of what remains and who must resolve it. Frequently asked questions

Do all crypto exchanges need a licence?

No, not every platform has the same licensing outcome. Requirements depend on the services it provides, how it handles transactions or assets, and the markets it serves. A software provider that doesn’t handle customer transactions may be assessed differently from a platform that executes trades or controls assets. Treat this as a jurisdiction-specific legal question, not a conclusion you can draw from the word “exchange” alone.

What activities should a crypto exchange include in its licensing assessment?

Assess every customer-facing function and any supporting service that affects a transaction or customer asset. The crypto exchange licensing checklist should capture features delivered by your company and services supplied through partners, including embedded products that may not appear in the platform’s main menu. For each one, note who contracts with the customer, who performs the service, and whether the function or provider changes between markets.

Can a crypto exchange operate in multiple countries with one licence?

Sometimes, but a licence doesn’t normally provide worldwide authorization. A licence may cover defined activities in its jurisdiction, while other countries can apply separate rules to customers they consider within scope. Under MiCA, an authorized CASP can passport its authorization across EU member states. That EU mechanism doesn’t extend to non-EU countries, so map each intended market and confirm whether local authorization or registration is required.

What documents are usually needed for a crypto exchange licence application?

There’s no universal document set. In addition to core company and operating materials, an application may require regulator-specific forms, declarations, or supporting records. Check the relevant authority’s current instructions for format, certification, translation, and submission requirements. Create a document register that marks each item as required, prepared, approved, or pending. This helps prevent an internal draft from being mistaken for a final, submission-ready document.

How long does crypto exchange licensing take?

There’s no reliable universal timeline. Preparation time depends on how quickly the business can resolve gaps and produce consistent evidence; review time depends on the jurisdiction and its process. Separate estimates into strategy, document preparation, submission, and regulator review. Don’t use a company-registration estimate as a proxy for authorization: forming an entity and receiving permission to conduct regulated activities are distinct milestones.

Does a crypto exchange licence automatically cover custody and derivatives?

No, don’t assume that an exchange authorization includes every related service. Custody and derivatives can receive separate treatment, depending on the jurisdiction, the product design, and which entity provides the service. The Panama licence activity scope lists 11 permitted activities: exchange, OTC desk, IEO, crypto-to-fiat, debit cards, fund, custodial services, futures and shorts, NFT minting and trading, token issuing, and staking. This scope shouldn’t be treated as a universal rule or assumed to authorize activities beyond those listed.

Is Panama a crypto exchange licensing option for businesses targeting the EU?

Panama can be considered for permitted crypto activities, but a Panama entity does not provide EU passporting. Businesses serving EU customers need to assess EU authorization separately. As of October 2026, the MiCA transitional period has expired, and entities serving EU residents need full MiCA CASP authorization. Evaluate the Panama structure and EU market access as distinct parts of the operating plan, based on the services and customers involved.
