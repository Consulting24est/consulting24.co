Operating a Crypto Business Without a License: Risks and Consequences

By Mardo Soo, Founder & CEO, Consulting24 (X24Consulting OÜ) · Updated 2026-10-24

Short answer: Learn the key operating a crypto business without a license risks, including regulatory enforcement, banking restrictions, and severe criminal AML penalties.

Operating without the required authorization can put a crypto business at risk before it scales. The risks of operating a crypto business without a license include enforcement action, restrictions on financial transactions, and administrative or criminal penalties where local law provides for them. Requirements depend on the activity and the markets served: registration, licensing, and exemptions vary by jurisdiction.

Incorporating a company or serving customers online does not settle the licensing question. A business may still need authorization for activities such as exchanging virtual currency, transmitting money, or holding customer assets. Its compliance duties may also include customer due diligence and sanctions screening.

This article explains how to assess rules market by market, how gaps in AML controls and supervision can compound licensing exposure, and when criminal penalties may apply. It also covers why exchange, wallet, custody, and token models can face different requirements, and what to document before deciding to launch in a market.

Key Takeaways

  • Map each crypto activity to the rules in every market you serve; company formation alone doesn’t establish permission to operate.
  • Treat authorization as an operational dependency, especially when your model relies on banking, custody, or fiat transaction rails.
  • Build customer due diligence and sanctions screening into your compliance controls, not as a substitute for required registration or licensing.
  • Distinguish FinCEN registration from state licensing: a crypto business may face both, depending on its activities and where it operates.
  • Assess enforcement and criminal exposure against the specific law and facts; proposed legislation is not the same as an enacted penalty.

What happens when a crypto business operates without the right registration or licensing in a regulated market?

Operating without required authorization can trigger enforcement, restrictions, and demands for remediation. The consequences depend on the activity, customer location, and applicable legislation.

Regulatory Enforcement and Remediation

Enforcement is regulatory action against activity conducted without required authorization. Regulators may restrict operations and require a business to address the underlying compliance failures. In California, the Department of Financial Protection and Innovation announced a $500,000 settlement with Nexo Capital over alleged unlicensed lending to California residents. The violations affected more than 5,400 residents.

Remediation may mean stopping affected services, correcting customer-facing processes, and documenting changes to controls. Assign clear responsibility for licensing, retain records, and track which activities are permitted in each market. Prompt, accurate engagement with regulators helps establish what action is required and which operations can continue.

Oversight and Customer Protections

Regulatory oversight sets operating conditions intended to protect customers and support lawful activity. An unlicensed cryptocurrency exchanger does not guarantee customer-interest protection or compliance with applicable regulations. Customers may be uncertain about how their assets are handled, while the business lacks the clarity and protections associated with an authorized operating framework.

For operators, customer safeguards need to connect to actual business controls. Document who can access digital assets, how customer complaints are handled, and which activities fall within each authorization. These records help identify where controls fail and make responsibilities visible. A strict approach matters because an operational gap can affect both customer treatment and regulatory standing.

Regulatory Uncertainty for Crypto Businesses

Regulatory uncertainty arises because countries define crypto activities differently and may update their rules. The same exchange, custody, or token model can face different requirements across markets. Laws and government policies also evolve, potentially affecting how digital assets are bought, sold, taxed, or used.

Before launch, document each target market’s licensing status and the precise scope of planned services. Treat missing authorization as a material go/no-go risk, not an issue to defer until customer growth. Revisit the analysis when legislation changes, the product adds features, or the business serves customers in a new location. This turns a changing regulatory environment into a defined operational decision.

MiCA in the European Union

The European Union’s MiCA Regulation brings cryptocurrency companies into the category of legitimate financial institutions. That shift makes the regulatory framework relevant to firms planning crypto activity in the EU. It also places authorization and compliance considerations within a more formal market structure.

For an EU expansion, define the planned services and markets before launch. Exchange, custody, and token activities may raise different questions. Map each activity to the applicable requirements, assign internal responsibility, and retain evidence of decisions. This supports regulatory readiness without assuming authorization in one jurisdiction settles obligations elsewhere.

The practical test is whether the business can show where it operates, what it does, and which authorization supports each activity. Keep that record current as products, customers, and legislation change. If a gap appears, pause the affected market activity and resolve the authorization and control issues before treating the launch as compliant.

AML, customer due diligence, sanctions screening, and supervision

Licensing and anti-money laundering controls address related but distinct risks. Authorization determines whether a business may conduct regulated activities in a jurisdiction. AML controls help identify customers, understand transactions, and detect possible illicit activity. A registration gap can therefore leave both market access and compliance responsibilities unresolved.

In the United States, the Bank Secrecy Act and FinCEN regulations apply to certain money-transmission and virtual-currency business models. Businesses transmitting virtual assets are generally Money Services Businesses and must register with FinCEN. This is a registration requirement, not a license. State money-transmitter licensing may also apply, depending on the activity and where the business operates.

Customer due diligence provides a structured way to identify users and assess transaction risks. Sanctions screening helps detect whether customers or counterparties may be restricted. Cryptocurrency transactions are generally pseudonymous, not anonymous. A digital address alone may not show who controls it, so a compliance framework should connect customer records, transaction monitoring, escalation decisions, and accountable staff.

Banking, Payment, Custody, and Partner Access

Authorization status can affect access to banking, payment, custody, and commercial partners. Treat it as an operational dependency, not paperwork to address after trading begins. Map each service and jurisdiction, then record which approvals and compliance controls support the activity. A partner’s involvement does not automatically settle the business’s own obligations.

DeFi Reporting and Self-Custody

DeFi design does not erase every regulatory question. In April 2025, Congress used the Congressional Review Act to repeal the IRS DeFi broker rule. That rule would have required front-end DeFi service providers to collect customer information and report transactions on Form 1099-DA. Its repeal concerns that reporting rule; it does not establish a general exemption from other requirements.

Self-custody differs from a service that accepts and transmits assets on a customer’s behalf. Assess who controls the activity, who interacts with users, and whether the business transmits or otherwise controls convertible virtual currency for customers. These functions can affect registration and Travel Rule obligations under the Bank Secrecy Act.

Digital Asset Custody and Supervision

Custody raises practical questions about asset control, access, and responsibility. Document who can authorize transfers, how customer assets are recorded, and how exceptions reach compliance staff. The FATF Recommendations shape how jurisdictions approach virtual asset activity, customer due diligence, beneficial ownership, and supervision. This framework helps explain why obligations differ across markets.

For a custody model, document the legal entity, customer-facing service, and control structure separately. Review the map whenever trading features, customer locations, or transaction flows change. Clear ownership of AML duties helps supervisors understand how the business identifies risks and responds to suspicious activity.

Criminal penalties for unlicensed virtual currency activity

Criminal exposure depends on the jurisdiction, the service offered, and the conduct involved. Unlicensed cryptocurrency exchanges may face administrative and criminal sanctions under applicable regulations. Distinguish a licensing breach from allegations involving fraud, money laundering, or other offenses, which have different elements and consequences. Handling virtual currency does not, by itself, establish criminal liability. The key question is whether the activity meets a regulated definition and was conducted without required authorization.

New York provides a current example of how proposed legislation could change exposure. The proposed CRYPTO Act would criminalize operating a cryptocurrency business without a license if enacted. It would require virtual currency businesses to hold a BitLicense issued by the New York State Department of Financial Services. Under the proposal, unlicensed activity involving more than $1 million in aggregate transactions over a 12-month period could qualify for a Class C felony charge.

The proposed penalty is substantial: a felony conviction could carry a prison term of five to 15 years. Those consequences belong to the proposed CRYPTO Act, not a law already in force. If enacted, the measure would make New York the 19th state to explicitly criminalize unlicensed crypto transactions. Operators should track whether legislation has passed before treating a proposal as an enforceable rule or assuming it creates no future exposure.

Criminal proceedings can also disrupt operations before a case reaches its final outcome. Investigations may require records explaining transaction flows, customer relationships, and who controlled the activity. Preserve licensing analyses, authorization documents, and clear descriptions of services offered. If a market review identifies a gap, stop the affected activity while assessing the relevant rules. A documented decision helps distinguish deliberate operation from a process failure, though it does not erase a legal breach.

For an exchange, wallet, or token business, assess each jurisdiction separately before launch. Identify the regulated activity, the responsible entity, and the authorization required to obtain before offering the service. Keep proposed legislation separate from current requirements in internal records. Escalate changes in product design, customer location, or transaction flows for review. This keeps compliance decisions tied to actual operations and reduces the risk of treating a general business registration as permission for every virtual currency activity.

Licensing and registration of cryptocurrency exchanges

U.S. crypto exchange requirements depend on the activity, transaction flow, and customer locations. No single state license automatically covers every platform. A business may need federal registration and state money-transmitter licenses, while securities or derivatives activity can raise separate questions. Map each service to the rules in every state where customers can access it. Operating a crypto business without a license risks regulatory action when the service crosses a state’s licensing threshold.

How do crypto licensing requirements vary by U.S. state? States define money transmission and virtual currency activity differently. Michigan’s Money Transmission Services Act of 2006 requires money transmitters to be licensed, while Utah’s cited provision excludes blockchain tokens from its definition of money transmission.

What state money transmitter licenses are required for crypto businesses? Requirements depend on whether the business receives, holds, or transmits money or virtual currency, and where it operates. For example, Alabama requires money transmitters to be licensed under Ala. Code § 8-7A-5; Maine includes receiving or transmitting virtual currencies under 32 ME Rev Stat § 6102.

Other state examples show why a state-by-state review matters. Alaska requires money-transmission licensing under AK Stat § 06.55.101, and Minnesota prohibits unlicensed money transmission under MN Stat § 53B.02. Oregon also prohibits conducting a money-transmission business without a license under OR Rev Stat § 717.205. These rules turn on the service’s function, not simply its “crypto exchange” label.

What exemptions or exceptions apply to crypto businesses under money transmission laws? Exemptions depend on the statute and business model. Indiana’s cited provision indicates an exchange may not need a license when it only enables digital-currency purchases or sales and does not allow fiat transfers between users. FinCEN also says an MSB acting solely as another MSB’s agent need not register.

Exemptions must be read against actual transaction flows. Colorado’s Digital Token Act of 2019 exempts certain crypto-related activities from state securities registration and broker-dealer and salesperson licensing requirements. That is not a blanket money-transmission exemption. Ohio requires applicants transacting in virtual currency to provide a current third-party security audit of relevant computer and information systems. Operating a crypto business without a license risks enforcement if an operator treats a narrow exception as broad permission.

How much do U.S. crypto business licenses cost and how long does approval take? Costs and timing depend on jurisdiction, business structure, activities, and application requirements; case-specific pricing is provided after a quote request. Approval timing also varies with the regulator’s process and the completeness of the application, so plan state filings around each authority’s requirements.

Before opening an exchange to a state, document its products, transaction paths, custody arrangements, and customer locations. Then identify each registration, license, or exemption supporting those activities. This record distinguishes a platform’s federal MSB status from its state licensing position and exposes gaps before they affect trading or partner access.

When does a crypto business qualify as a money services business under FinCEN rules?

In the United States, a crypto business may qualify as a Money Services Business (MSB) when its activities involve transmitting virtual assets. The analysis turns on what the business does for customers, not its company name or technology. Registration with the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) is a federal requirement, not a state license.

Start with the transaction flow. Does the business accept convertible virtual currency from one person and transmit it to another? Does it otherwise control those assets on a customer’s behalf? These functions can bring a business within money-transmission obligations under the Bank Secrecy Act. A platform that only publishes software may present different facts from a service that controls customer transfers.

FinCEN’s MSB analysis is separate from state licensing. Federal registration does not replace state-by-state money-transmitter licensing where required. Nor does a state license settle federal status. Keep separate records identifying the activity, the entity performing it, the customer relationship, and the relevant jurisdiction. This avoids treating one approval as coverage for every operation.

The distinction matters for exchanges, custodial wallets, and hosted transaction services. For example, an exchange that receives customer virtual currency and transmits it to another party may have different obligations from a platform that merely matches buyers and sellers without taking control. The actual custody and transfer arrangement matters more than the product label.

The Travel Rule applies to businesses qualifying as money transmitters under the Bank Secrecy Act when they accept and transmit, or otherwise control, convertible virtual currency on a customer’s behalf. Assess whether the business controls the transfer, then map the related customer and transaction-information processes. Do not assume that using blockchain or a self-hosted wallet removes obligations tied to the business’s own transmission activity.

There is a defined agent exception: according to the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), a person that is an MSB solely because it acts as an agent of another MSB is not required to register. The exception depends on the person’s role and why it qualifies as an MSB. It does not automatically resolve state licensing or other compliance duties.

For each product, document who receives the assets, who controls the transfer, and who sends them onward. Reassess the analysis when a platform adds custody, changes transaction routing, or serves a new customer group. These operational changes can alter federal classification even when the company and its branding stay the same.

Frequently asked questions

Which U.S. crypto business activities require federal or state licenses?

Requirements depend on the service, transaction flow, and customer locations. Transmitting virtual assets can trigger federal Money Services Business registration, while money transmission may require state licenses. Exchange, custody, token, securities, and derivatives activities can raise separate regulatory questions. For example, a platform handling customer transfers may be assessed differently from one that only provides software. Classify each product function before deciding which registrations or licenses apply.

What are the penalties for operating an unlicensed crypto exchange or money transmitter?

Consequences depend on applicable law and may include administrative action, operational restrictions, or criminal sanctions where statutes allow them. Financial transactions may also be blocked, disrupting customer access and business operations. No single national penalty applies to every exchange or money transmitter. Relevant factors include where the service operates, what it does, and whether an exemption applies under that jurisdiction’s rules.

Can business owners face criminal charges for unlicensed crypto operations?

Yes, owners can face criminal charges if authorities allege their conduct meets an applicable criminal offense. A company’s unlicensed status alone does not establish an individual’s guilt. Relevant evidence may include who directed the service, approved transactions, or controlled compliance decisions. Owners should preserve records showing roles and authorization decisions, and distinguish enacted requirements from proposed legislation when assessing potential exposure.

What civil enforcement actions can the SEC or CFTC bring against unlicensed crypto businesses?

The SEC and CFTC oversee different parts of the U.S. crypto market, so the activity determines which agency may have jurisdiction. Securities offerings generally require registration unless an exemption applies, while the Commodity Exchange Act governs CFTC oversight of derivatives and related activity involving commodities. Assess offering and trading functions separately. If enacted, the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act would allocate oversight differently.

What steps should a crypto startup take to determine and obtain required licenses?

Start by documenting each product function, transaction path, customer location, and responsible entity. Then map those activities against federal registration rules, state licensing laws, and applicable exemptions. Record the legal basis for each market decision and identify any activity without a clear authorization path. Reassess the map before adding custody, changing transfer flows, or opening service to customers in another state.

What risks do customers face when using an unlicensed crypto business?

Customers may have less clarity about how their assets are handled and what protections apply if a dispute arises. An unlicensed exchanger does not guarantee customer-interest protection or compliance with applicable regulations. Customers should understand who controls transfers, how balances are recorded, and what complaint process exists. Digital assets also face market volatility, scams, fraud, and hacking risks, which are distinct from licensing status.
