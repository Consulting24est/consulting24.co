Estonia vs Netherlands Crowdfunding License (2026): the 75x Fee Gap

By Mardo Soo, Founder & CEO, Consulting24 (X24Consulting OÜ) · Updated 2026-08-01

Short answer: Estonia charges a flat EUR 1,000 to process an ECSPR crowdfunding application. The Dutch AFM bills the same application at EUR 200 per hour, capped at EUR 75,000 — a 75x gap in the worst case — and a complete Dutch project typically passes EUR 100,000 (industry estimate). Both licenses give identical EU-wide access: a free Article 18 passport with a 15-calendar-day clock. The honest trade-off: the Netherlands has the deeper home market (20 platforms on the AFM register), Estonia the cheaper, emptier, more attentive seat.

Estonia and the Netherlands issue exactly the same license. Under Regulation (EU) 2020/1503 (ECSPR), a crowdfunding service provider authorization from any member state covers lending-based and investment-based crowdfunding for business projects up to EUR 5,000,000 per project owner per 12 months, in all 27 member states. The regulation fixes the capital rule, the review clocks and the passport. What it deliberately leaves to each country is the price of admission and the temperament of the regulator — and on that line, Estonia and the Netherlands sit at opposite ends of the EU.

Consulting24 delivers the Estonian route directly — full application preparation and Estonian-language filing with Finantsinspektsioon (Estonian Financial Supervision Authority) at EUR 37,000 fixed — and this page compares that route honestly against the AFM's. If the Dutch market itself is the prize, we say so below.

The 75x fee gap, explained

Estonia's number is simple: Finantsinspektsioon charges a EUR 1,000 processing fee with the application, and EUR 0 for each Article 18 cross-border notification afterwards. That is the entire state-fee bill for an EU-wide license.

The Dutch number is a meter, not a price. Under the Ministry of Finance tariff, the AFM bills ECSPR applicants for its assessment time at EUR 200 per hour, up to a cap of EUR 75,000 (AFM application page, retrieved August 2026). Three consequences follow:

  • A weak first filing literally costs money. Every completeness question, every clarification round, every re-read of a revised business-continuity plan is billable time. In Estonia a sloppy file costs you calendar months; in the Netherlands it costs months and an open-ended invoice.
  • The bill is unknowable in advance. Between EUR 200 (one theoretical hour) and EUR 75,000 sits your actual invoice, and you find out at the end. Budgeting conservatively means budgeting near the cap.
  • Entry friction starts before the fee. The AFM application form is not a download or a portal — it is only obtainable by emailing CSPvergunningaanvraag@afm.nl and requesting it.

And the hourly meter is not the whole Dutch cost. Industry cost roundups put a complete Netherlands crowdfunding-licensing project at “EUR 100,000 and more” (LenderKit, industry estimate) once per-person suitability assessments (around EUR 700 per person) and fitness assessments (from EUR 175/hour) are included — before Dutch counsel, Dutch salaries or Dutch office costs. For the full Estonian equivalent, itemized line by line, see the cost of a crowdfunding license in Estonia — the professional-plus-state total is EUR 38,000.

Head-to-head: Estonia vs Netherlands ECSPR license

The ECSPR constants are identical everywhere — own funds of EUR 25,000 (or one quarter of prior-year fixed overheads, if higher, or an EU-covering insurance policy, per Art 11), a 25-working-day completeness check plus a 3-month decision clock on a complete file (Art 12), the EUR 5M per-project-owner cap, and the free 15-day Art 18 passport. What the Netherlands genuinely does better

Honesty first: of the jurisdictions we compare Estonia against — Lithuania, Latvia, Ireland and the Netherlands — the Dutch market is the deepest. The AFM register lists 20 licensed crowdfunding platforms (register PDF dated 1 June 2026), the fourth-largest count in the EU behind France, Italy and Spain. Behind that number sits what the count implies: a large domestic base of retail investors who already understand crowdfunding, project owners who already use it, and an ecosystem of Dutch banks, lawyers and payment providers who have onboarded platforms before.

The AFM itself is a strict, thorough, internationally respected supervisor. If your platform's story depends on Dutch institutional partnerships or on “AFM-licensed” as a domestic trust badge, that has real value — and no Estonian license substitutes for it in a Dutch pitch deck. A Dutch-founded platform raising Dutch money for Dutch SMEs, with EUR 100,000+ of entry budget and patience for an hourly-billed review, has a perfectly rational case for licensing at home.

The counterpoint in one sentence: 20 incumbents means you are platform #21 in the Netherlands, while Estonia's register holds exactly one active provider (checked August 2026) — and the license you receive grants access to the same 27 markets either way.

License in Tallinn, passport into Amsterdam for EUR 0

Here is the structural point that decides most cases: you do not need a Dutch license to serve the Dutch market. Under ECSPR Article 18, an Estonian-authorized platform files a cross-border notification through Finantsinspektsioon listing the member states it intends to serve. The fee is EUR 0. Services may start at the latest 15 calendar days after the notification is submitted. The same mechanism covers France, Germany and the other 24 markets — each additional country is another free line on the same notification.

This is not a theoretical workaround; it is how the Estonian seat is actually used. Per analysis of ESMA country data, roughly 78% of amounts invested through Estonian-authorized providers comes from outside Estonia (first-party analysis of country data underlying the ESMA Market Report on Crowdfunding, 2024 data) — among the highest cross-border shares in the EU. The wider market you are passporting into: 181 authorized CSPs across 21 member states raised over EUR 4bn in 2024, per the same ESMA report. The Estonian license was built for exactly this “small home base, EU-wide distribution” pattern.

The cap is the same from either seat: EUR 5,000,000 per project owner per rolling 12 months. Above that, you are in prospectus/MiFID territory regardless of where you licensed. And crowdfunding raises in tokenized instruments fall outside ECSPR entirely — that is MiCA/CASP territory.

Which should you choose?

Choose Estonia if… your investors and project owners are spread across the EU rather than concentrated in the Netherlands; you want the entry bill to be a known EUR 38,000 (professional + state) instead of an open-ended hourly meter plus EUR 100,000+ of project cost; you value being one of very few applications on a senior supervisor's desk rather than platform #21; you want to launch equity crowdfunding into a register where no equity platform has ever been licensed (checked August 2026); or you want 0% corporate tax on retained profits while you scale.

Choose the Netherlands if… the Dutch home market itself is your business case — Dutch retail investors, Dutch SME deal flow, Dutch institutional partners who expect an AFM license; your budget comfortably absorbs the EUR 75,000 fee cap and a EUR 100,000+ total project (industry estimate); and your founding team can carry a Dutch-substance operation. The AFM's thoroughness is a feature for that strategy, not a bug — it is simply an expensive feature.

A third pattern is common and legitimate: license in Estonia now, passport into the Netherlands on day one for EUR 0, and revisit a Dutch entity only if Dutch volume ever justifies EUR 100,000+ of domestic infrastructure. The reverse order — paying the AFM meter first, then discovering your volume was pan-European anyway — is the expensive way to learn the same lesson.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a crowdfunding license cost in the Netherlands compared to Estonia?

The Dutch AFM bills the ECSPR application at EUR 200 per hour, capped at EUR 75,000, under the Ministry of Finance tariff — and industry cost roundups put a full Dutch licensing project at EUR 100,000 or more once fitness assessments (from EUR 175/hour) and per-person checks (around EUR 700 per person) are added. Estonia charges a flat EUR 1,000 processing fee to Finantsinspektsioon. On the state-fee line alone, the worst-case gap is 75x.

Can I serve Dutch investors with an Estonian ECSPR license?

Yes. ECSPR is one EU-wide regime: after authorization in Estonia, an Article 18 cross-border notification filed through Finantsinspektsioon costs EUR 0, and you can start serving the Netherlands at the latest 15 calendar days after submitting it. You get the same legal access to Dutch project owners and investors as an AFM-licensed platform, up to EUR 5,000,000 per project owner per 12 months.

Is the Dutch AFM faster than Finantsinspektsioon?

Both regulators run the same statutory clocks under ECSPR Article 12: a completeness check within 25 working days and a decision within 3 months of a complete application. In Estonia, realistic elapsed time is around 6-9 months including Q&A rounds. The AFM does not publish elapsed-time data for crowdfunding applications, so Dutch practice timelines are unverified — and because the AFM bills by the hour, every extra review round also costs money.

What is the capital requirement for a crowdfunding license in Estonia and the Netherlands?

Identical, because it is set by ECSPR Article 11, not national law: own funds of EUR 25,000 (or one quarter of prior-year fixed overheads, if higher), which can be replaced by an insurance policy covering the EU territories where you market offers, or a combination of both. Insurance replacing own funds runs approximately EUR 1,000-1,500 per month — an indicative figure from recent client quotes, subject to insurer underwriting.

Why does the Netherlands bill the crowdfunding application hourly?

The AFM charges applicants for assessment time under a Ministry of Finance tariff: EUR 200 per hour, up to the EUR 75,000 cap. The practical consequence is that a weak or incomplete first filing literally costs money — every clarification round adds billable hours. The application form itself is only available by emailing the AFM (CSPvergunningaanvraag@afm.nl); there is no open download or online portal.

Which country is better for an equity crowdfunding platform?

The Netherlands has the deeper market: 20 crowdfunding service providers on the AFM register (register PDF dated 1 June 2026) and a mature domestic investor base. Estonia has the emptier field: as of August 2026, Finantsinspektsioon's register lists one authorised provider (lending-based), and no equity platform has ever been licensed there. If you want incumbent-free regulator attention and a flat EUR 1,000 fee, Estonia; if you want to sit inside an established Dutch ecosystem and can absorb EUR 100,000+ in entry costs, the Netherlands.
